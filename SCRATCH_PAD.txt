CROSS_CHECKING
A QSO looks like this:
QSO: 14252 PH 2023-04-01 1514 AA0FO         59  KS      K5LSU         59  EBR
the CHECK QSO would look like this:
QSO: 14252 PH 2023-04-01 1517 K5LSU         59  EBR     AA0FO         59  KS

General approach. Process all QSO's as if they cross-checked and write their results into the DB. A match for this QSO is looked for in the "xcheck" table. If there was no match in the table, or the two QSOs fail to match, record that fact. If they do match, then don't record anything for the sending op and remove any record of this QSO for the receiving op in the table.

Once all logs are processed, we need to go through the xcheck and for each callsign in there, we need to adjust their stats in the DB. We reduce qso points and potentially mults

The two contacts must match EXACTLY on the two callsigns and the frequency. So when encountering the first QSO, we need to look for a QSO in the cross-check Dict table of seen QSOs that has this key: remote operator's callsign. The value for this key is a list of Dict's. Each element in te list represents a QSO of his that was processed without a corresponding cross-check QSO. The keys in each Dict are receiving callsign, time (not date), and whether this QSO was provisionally couned as a MULT in addition to its QSO points.

If that record is in the table, we check that the times are within 10 minutes (???) of each other. 
IF they are, then the QSO currently beoing processed "CHECKS" and can count for points. The record for the QSO of the receiving operator is removed. If that was the last un-matched QSO for that callsign, the entire record for that callsign is removed.

If they do not check, then we do a few things:
(1) count this QSO for the current user for points and mults and continue processing.

It appears that to get the liability protection we want, we must technically give the GNOF "variance power" (ultimate legal control). In practice, foundations like GNOF will usually follow the client’s advice to release funds for our mission (repairing towers, etc.), but they legally must have the final say to validate the transfer of assets.

It also seems that this approach is not 100% effective in shielding assets. Even though we would not legally own and control the funds, accounting rules (FASB ASC 958) often require us to still list them as an asset ("Beneficial Interest in Assets Held by Others") on our own financial statements. This means a savvy plaintiff's lawyer might see them on your balance sheet and try to argue they are available assets, even if legally they aren't.
